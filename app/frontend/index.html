Learn RAG (concepts)

  
A plain-English walkthrough of Retrieval-Augmented Generation, using the exact stages you can click through in the Input, Query, and RAG Eval tabs as live, inspectable examples.


  

    

      

        
The problem RAG solves

      

    

    

      A large language model (like qwen2.5:7b, used in Stage 12) only knows what it learned during
      training - it has never seen your PDF. Ask it a question about your document directly and it will
      either say it doesn't know, or worse, confidently make something up (a "hallucination").
    

    

      RAG's fix: before asking the LLM to answer, first retrieve
      the specific pieces of your document that are actually relevant to the question, then hand those
      pieces to the LLM as reference material and say "answer using only this." The LLM stops relying on
      memory and starts reading - closer to an open-book exam than a pop quiz.
    

    

      Analogy: a brilliant new employee who has never read your company's documents. RAG is the act of
      handing them exactly the right three pages before they answer your question, instead of asking them
      to answer from memory.
    

  


  

    

      

        
The two-phase shape of every RAG system

        
This app makes both phases visible as separate tabs - most production RAG tools hide this split entirely.

      

    

    

      

        
📄

        
Indexing phase

        
Runs ONCE per document, ahead of time. Turns a PDF into searchable pieces sitting in a database. Nothing about the user's question happens here.

      

      
=>

      

        
🔍

        
Query phase

        
Runs EVERY TIME a user asks a question. Searches the already-indexed pieces, then generates a grounded answer.

      

    

    

      In this app: the Input tab is the indexing phase (Stages 1-4 -
      you do this once per document). The Query tab is the query phase
      (Stages 6-12 - you do this every time you ask a new question). The split matters because indexing
      is slow and expensive (parsing, embedding) but only needs to happen once, while querying needs to
      be fast since a real user is waiting on it.
    

  


  

    

      

        
Indexing phase - turning a document into something searchable

      

    


    

      Stage 1-2 · Input tab
      
Parse, then chunk

      
A PDF is not naturally searchable - it's a flat blob of text and layout. Docling first converts
      it to structured Markdown that preserves headings, tables, and reading order (Stage 1). Then the
      chunker splits that into small pieces of a few hundred words each, called "chunks" (Stage 2) -
      never the whole document at once.

      
Why not just search the whole document? Because later, the LLM can only
      read a limited amount of text at once (its "context window"), and because a shorter, focused chunk
      is easier to match precisely against a question than an entire 50-page document.

    


    

      Stage 3 · Input tab
      
Embed - turning text into numbers

      
An embedding model (bge-m3) reads each chunk and outputs a list of 1024 numbers - a "vector" -
      that represents the chunk's meaning, not its exact words. Chunks about similar topics end up
      with similar numbers, even if they don't share any of the same words.

      
Analogy: imagine plotting every sentence you've ever read as a dot on a
      map, where dots close together mean similar meaning. "The car is fast" and "the vehicle is quick"
      would land near each other, even though they share almost no words.

    


    

      Stage 4 · Input tab
      
Store - saving it for later

      
Every chunk's text and its vector get saved into Postgres (using the pgvector extension, which
      knows how to store and search vectors efficiently). This is what makes indexing a one-time cost -
      once stored, you can search this document as many times as you want without re-parsing or
      re-embedding it.

    

  


  

    

      

        
Query phase - answering a real question

      

    


    

      Stage 6 · Query tab
      
Normalize the question

      
Before searching, the raw question gets lightly cleaned up - extra whitespace collapsed, invisible
      characters stripped. Small, but it stops "  AEPS   error 91 " and "AEPS error 91" from being treated
      as two different questions later in the pipeline.

    


    

      Stage 7 · Query tab
      
Semantic search - the core of "retrieval"

      
The question itself gets embedded with the same bge-m3 model used during indexing, turning it into
      a vector too. Then pgvector finds the stored chunks whose vectors are closest to the question's
      vector - "closest" measured by cosine similarity. This is retrieval finding meaning-based matches,
      not keyword matches.

      
This is the step that lets "how much can I borrow" match a chunk that
      says "maximum loan eligibility" - no shared words, but close in meaning.

    


    

      Stage 8 · Query tab
      
Keyword search - semantic search's blind spot

      
Semantic search is weak at exact terms: product codes, error numbers, specific names. PostgreSQL's
      full-text search (Stage 8) does old-fashioned lexical matching instead - no meaning involved, just
      "does this exact word appear." The two methods cover each other's weaknesses.

    


    

      Stage 9 · Query tab
      
Hybrid search - combining both rankings

      
Semantic search and keyword search produce two different ranked lists, scored on scales that
      can't be compared directly. Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) combines them by rank position instead of
      raw score - a chunk that shows up near the top of either list gets boosted, and a chunk that
      ranks well in both gets boosted even more. This is what "hybrid search" means in most
      production RAG systems.

    


    

      Stage 10 · Query tab
      
No-answer detection - knowing when to say "I don't know"

      
Retrieval always returns something - even for a question with no real answer in the
      document, the search still returns its "closest" matches, which may just be noise. Stage 10 checks
      whether those top matches are actually good enough to trust before letting the LLM try to answer
      from them. This is what stops the app from confidently answering an out-of-scope question.

      
This app's build hit a real bug here worth knowing about: scoring only
      by RRF rank couldn't tell "a real answer found by just one search method" apart from "garbage found
      by neither" - both scored identically. The fix added a second, independent check (raw similarity)
      as a backup signal. Retrieval quality checks like this are a core, easy-to-underestimate part of
      building RAG for real.

    


    

      Stage 11 · Query tab
      
Context construction - preparing evidence for the LLM

      
The surviving chunks get cleaned up before handing them to the LLM: duplicates removed, chunks
      that are next to each other in the source document merged into one coherent block, put back into
      reading order (not search-rank order), trimmed to fit a token budget, and tagged with citation
      markers like [S1], [S2] so the answer can point back to its sources.

    


    

      Stage 12 · Query tab
      
Generation - the "G" in RAG

      
Only now does the LLM (qwen2.5:7b) get involved. It receives the original question plus the
      prepared context blocks, with explicit instructions to answer only from what's provided and to cite
      sources. This is the one step people usually mean when they say "AI answers a question" - but as
      you can see, it's the last of seven steps, not the whole system.

    


    

      Stage 13 · RAG Eval tab
      
Evaluation - proving it actually works

      
A single good-looking answer doesn't prove a RAG pipeline is reliable. Stage 13 runs a whole set
      of known questions with known-correct expectations through the full pipeline automatically, scoring
      retrieval accuracy, no-answer detection, and answer quality separately - because a wrong answer
      could mean retrieval found the wrong chunk, or it could mean the LLM misread the right chunk. Without
      separating those, you can't tell which part of the system to fix.

How this app helps you actually learn RAG, not just use it

      

    

    

      Most RAG tutorials and frameworks bundle all thirteen of these steps into a single function call -
      you get an answer, but never see what happened in between. That's fine for shipping a product
      quickly, but it hides exactly the parts worth understanding: why retrieval sometimes returns the
      wrong chunk, why an LLM can still get the right context and give a wrong answer, and why "it worked
      in my demo" is not the same as "it's reliable."
    

    

      This app deliberately keeps every stage as its own button with its own visible output - so you can
      run Stage 7 and Stage 8 separately and see them disagree, watch Stage 9 resolve that disagreement,
      watch Stage 10 catch a bad retrieval before it reaches the LLM, and run Stage 13 to prove (or
      disprove) that a fix actually improved things. That's the whole point: RAG is not one black box, it
      is a short pipeline of individually-understandable, individually-testable decisions.
